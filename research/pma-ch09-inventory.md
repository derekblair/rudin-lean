## Linear transformations

- **PMA-9.1** - definitions, pp.204-205: Vector subspaces of real Euclidean space; finite linear combinations and span; linear independence/dependence; dimension as the maximum size of an independent family; basis and coordinates. Include that a span is a subspace, an independent family has no zero vector, the zero subspace has dimension zero, coordinates in a basis exist uniquely, and the standard coordinate vectors form the usual basis.
- **PMA-9.2** - theorem/corollary, pp.205-206: A space spanned by `r > 0` vectors has dimension at most `r`. Attached corollary: real Euclidean `n`-space has dimension `n`.
- **PMA-9.3** - theorem, p.206: In a space of dimension `n`, a family of `n` vectors spans exactly when it is independent; a basis exists and every basis has `n` elements; an independent family of `r` vectors, `1 <= r <= n`, extends to a basis.
- **PMA-9.4** - definitions/consequences, pp.206-207: Linear transformations preserve sums and scalar multiples. Include preservation of zero, the action on a finite linear combination, and determination by the images of a basis. An invertible endomorphism is a bijective linear map; its inverse is linear and satisfies both inverse identities.
- **PMA-9.5** - theorem, p.207: An endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space is injective if and only if it is surjective.
- **PMA-9.6** - definitions/consequences, pp.207-208: Operator addition and scalar multiplication act pointwise, and the product `BA` means `B` after `A`; these are linear maps, and composition need not commute. Define the Euclidean operator norm using the closed unit ball. Include `norm (A x) <= norm A * norm x` and the least-bound property.
- **PMA-9.7** - theorem, pp.208-209: Every linear map between finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces has finite norm and is uniformly continuous. Operator norms satisfy the triangle inequality and scalar homogeneity; `dist A B = norm (A-B)` defines a metric. Composition satisfies `norm (BA) <= norm B * norm A`.
- **PMA-9.8** - theorem, p.209: If `A` is invertible and `norm (B-A) * norm (A^-1) < 1`, then `B` is invertible. The invertible operators form an open set, and inversion is continuous there. Attached observation: inversion is a bijection of this set and is its own inverse.
- **PMA-9.9** - matrices/discussion, pp.210-211: Given bases of domain and codomain, a linear map corresponds bijectively to its matrix; column `j` records the image of basis vector `j`, and the range is spanned by those images. Include the coordinate action formula and the matrix-product formula for composition. For standard Euclidean bases, the operator norm is at most the square root of the sum of squared entries. Attached consequence: continuous real matrix entries, parametrized by any metric space, give a continuous operator-valued map.

## Differentiation

- **PMA-9.10** - preliminaries, pp.211-212: Interpret the scalar derivative and the derivative of a curve as a linear approximation with remainder `o(abs h)`. A linear map from the real line is multiplication of the input by its value at `1`; this identifies scalar derivatives with real endomorphisms and vector derivatives with maps from the real line. Include the equivalent normalized norm-remainder limit.
- **PMA-9.11** - definition, pp.212-213: For `f : E -> R^m`, with `E` open in `R^n` and `x in E`, a derivative is a linear map `A` satisfying `norm (f(x+h)-f(x)-A h) / norm h -> 0` as nonzero `h -> 0`. Define differentiability at a point and throughout `E`. Ambient extensions of `f` are acceptable when local domain equivalence is explicit.
- **PMA-9.12** - theorem, p.213: The linear map in the derivative definition is unique.
- **PMA-9.13** - remarks, p.213: Differentiability is equivalently a linear increment plus a remainder of norm `o(norm h)`; the derivative is an operator-valued function. Differentiability implies continuity. The total derivative/differential terminology is distinguished from coordinate partial derivatives.
- **PMA-9.14** - example, p.214: The derivative of a linear transformation at every point is that same transformation, with identically zero remainder.
- **PMA-9.15** - theorem, pp.214-215: Chain rule for differentiable maps between open subsets of Euclidean spaces: the derivative of `g` after `f` is `g'(f(x))` composed with `f'(x)`. Retain the domain/range-neighborhood hypotheses or an explicitly equivalent local formulation.
- **PMA-9.16** - partial derivatives/discussion, p.215: Components are coordinates in the standard output basis; the partial derivative of component `i` in direction `j` is the one-variable coordinate-line difference-quotient limit. Attached warnings: existence of every partial derivative does not ensure continuity, and even continuity together with existing partials does not ensure total differentiability. The referenced exercises are excluded, but these logical nonimplications are part of the numbered discussion.
- **PMA-9.17** - theorem/consequences, pp.215-216: Total differentiability implies existence of all component partial derivatives. The derivative applied to coordinate vector `j` has those partial derivatives as its coordinates. Include the derivative-matrix entries and its action on an arbitrary vector as the corresponding finite double sum.
- **PMA-9.18** - example/development, pp.216-218: For a differentiable curve `gamma` into an open set and a differentiable scalar function `f`, the derivative of `f(gamma(t))` is the sum of component partials times the corresponding curve derivatives. Define the gradient and express the same result by its inner product with the curve velocity. For a unit direction `u`, the directional quotient tends to `gradient f(x) dot u`, also equal to the coordinate sum. When the gradient is nonzero, the maximum directional derivative on the unit sphere is attained in the positive gradient direction.
- **PMA-9.19** - theorem/corollary, p.218: A differentiable map on a convex open set whose derivative norm is bounded by a real `M` satisfies `norm (f(b)-f(a)) <= M * norm (b-a)` for every pair of points in that set. Attached corollary: zero derivative throughout such a set implies constancy on the set.
- **PMA-9.20** - definition, p.219: A map is continuously differentiable (`C^1`) on an open set when it is differentiable there and its derivative is continuous in operator norm. Include the pointwise epsilon-delta formulation of derivative continuity.
- **PMA-9.21** - theorem, pp.219-220: A map on an open Euclidean set is `C^1` if and only if all component coordinate partial derivatives exist and are continuous there. The reverse implication must derive total differentiability rather than assume it.

## Contraction and inverse function theorems

- **PMA-9.22** - definition, p.220: A contraction is a metric-space self-map satisfying `dist (phi x) (phi y) <= c * dist x y` for some real `c < 1`. A nonnegative normalized constant is equivalent; the source's nonempty-space convention must be respected where existence is asserted.
- **PMA-9.23** - theorem, pp.220-221: A contraction on a nonempty complete metric space has exactly one fixed point. The attached description of the construction gives convergence of successive iterates from an arbitrary starting point. The geometric estimates inside the proof are useful strengthenings, not separate numbered nodes.
- **PMA-9.24** - theorem/remark, pp.221-223: A `C^1` map on an open subset of `R^n` with invertible derivative at `a` has an open neighborhood `U` of `a` on which it is a bijection onto an open neighborhood `V` of `f(a)`; the inverse `g` is `C^1`, and `g'(y) = (f'(g(y)))^-1` on `V`. Include two-sided local inverse identities and `U` contained in the original domain. Attached weaker version: differentiability throughout the open domain and continuity of the derivative only at `a` suffice for a differentiable local inverse and the derivative formula; full derivative continuity is needed for the stated `C^1` conclusion.
- **PMA-9.25** - theorem/discussion, p.223: A `C^1` map with invertible derivative at every point of its open domain maps each open subset of that domain to an open set. It is locally injective; global injectivity does not follow (the particular referenced exercise is excluded).

## Implicit functions and rank

- **PMA-9.26** - notation, p.224: Split `R^(n+m)` into an `x` block of size `n` and a `y` block of size `m`. For a linear map into `R^n`, define `A_x h = A(h,0)` and `A_y k = A(0,k)` and establish `A(h,k) = A_x h + A_y k`.
- **PMA-9.27** - theorem, p.224: If `A_x` is invertible, for each `k` there is exactly one `h` with `A(h,k)=0`; it is `-A_x^-1(A_y k)`. Include linearity of the solution as a function of `k`.
- **PMA-9.28** - theorem/consequence, pp.224-227: A `C^1` map `f` from an open subset of `R^(n+m)` to `R^n`, with `f(a,b)=0` and invertible `x` block of `f'(a,b)`, admits open neighborhoods `U` of `(a,b)` and `W` of `b` such that each `y in W` has a unique `x` with `(x,y) in U` and `f(x,y)=0`. The resulting `g : W -> R^n` is `C^1`, satisfies `g(b)=a` and `f(g(y),y)=0`, and has derivative `g'(b)=-A_x^-1 A_y`. Include the component identities `sum_j (partial f_i / partial x_j)(a,b) * (partial g_j / partial y_k)(b) = -(partial f_i / partial y_k)(a,b)`.
- **PMA-9.29** - example, pp.227-228: For `f_1=2 exp(x_1)+x_2*y_1-4*y_2+3` and `f_2=x_2*cos(x_1)-6*x_1+2*y_1-y_3`, take `a=(0,1)` and `b=(3,2,7)`. Verify `f(a,b)=0`, the derivative rows `[2,3,1,-4,0]` and `[-6,1,2,0,-1]`, and invertibility of the leading block with inverse `(1/20)*[[1,-3],[6,2]]`. Obtain the local `C^1` implicit function with value `a` and defining identity. Its derivative at `b` has rows `[1/4,1/5,-3/20]` and `[-1/2,6/5,1/10]`, giving all six component partial derivatives.
- **PMA-9.30** - definitions/consequences, p.228: Kernel and range of a linear map are subspaces; rank is the dimension of its range. Endomorphisms of `R^n` are invertible exactly when their rank is `n`; rank zero means the map is zero and its kernel is the whole space.
- **PMA-9.31** - projections, pp.228-229: A projection is a linear endomorphism `P` with `P^2=P`, equivalently one fixing its range. Every vector has a unique decomposition into a vector in the range plus one in the kernel. Every subspace of a finite-dimensional space is the range of a projection. Attached observation: over the reals there are infinitely many such projections when the subspace dimension is strictly between zero and the ambient dimension.
- **PMA-9.32** - theorem/geometric consequences, pp.229-231: Let a `C^1` map `F:E -> R^m`, on open `E` in `R^n`, have constant derivative rank `r`, where `0 <= r <= min(m,n)`. At `a`, put `A=F'(a)`. For any projection `P` onto `Y_1=range A`, with kernel `Y_2`, there are open `U` and `V` in `R^n`, with `a in U` and `U subset E`, a `C^1` bijection `H:V -> U` whose inverse is `C^1`, and a `C^1` map `phi:A(V) -> Y_2`, with `A(V)` open relative to `Y_1`, such that `F(H(x))=A x+phi(A x)`. Include rank zero. Attached consequences: `F(U)` is the graph of `phi` under the `Y_1 + Y_2` decomposition; `P` restricts to a bijection `F(U) -> A(V)`; the level sets of `F after H` in `V` are exactly those of `A`, which are intersections with translates of `kernel A`; `dim(kernel A)=n-r`; level sets of `F` are the corresponding images under `H`.

## Determinants

- **PMA-9.33** - definition, p.232: For an ordered integer tuple, define its sign factor by the product of signs of all later-minus-earlier differences; it is in `{-1,0,1}` and changes sign when distinct positions are swapped. The determinant is the finite sum over all tuples of column indices of this factor times one entry from each row. Repeated-index terms vanish. Include the equivalent interpretation as a function of the column vectors; a permutation-sum implementation needs an explicit bridge to this all-tuple formula.
- **PMA-9.34** - theorem, p.232: The identity matrix has determinant one; the determinant is linear separately in each column; interchanging two distinct columns negates it; equal distinct columns imply determinant zero.
- **PMA-9.35** - theorem, p.233: Determinants multiply under matrix multiplication: `det (B*A) = det B * det A`.
- **PMA-9.36** - theorem, pp.233-234: A real finite-dimensional endomorphism is invertible if and only if its matrix determinant is nonzero.
- **PMA-9.37** - remark, p.234: For a basis change represented by `B`, the old and new matrices of an operator satisfy `B*A_new=A_old*B`. Their determinants agree, so the determinant of an operator is independent of the chosen basis.
- **PMA-9.38** - Jacobians, p.234: The Jacobian at a differentiability point is the determinant of the total derivative. The inverse function theorem's nondegeneracy is equivalent to a nonzero Jacobian; the implicit function theorem's block-invertibility condition is equivalent to a nonzero partial Jacobian in the solved variables.

## Higher derivatives and differentiation of integrals

- **PMA-9.39** - definition/discussion, p.235: Define second coordinate partials by `D_ij f = D_i(D_j f)`, with this order explicit. A scalar function is `C^2` when these derivatives exist and are continuous; vector maps use the componentwise condition. Mixed partial derivatives may exist and be unequal without the continuity condition (the particular exercise is excluded).
- **PMA-9.40** - theorem, p.235: Suppose `D_1 f` and `D_21 f` exist throughout an open set in the plane. For a closed coordinate rectangle contained in it, with opposite vertices `(a,b)` and `(a+h,b+k)` and nonzero `h,k`, the rectangular increment `f(a+h,b+k)-f(a+h,b)-f(a,b+k)+f(a,b)` equals `h*k*D_21 f(x,y)` at some interior point. Either increment may be negative; no continuity of the mixed partial is assumed.
- **PMA-9.41** - theorem/corollary, pp.235-236: Suppose `D_1 f`, `D_21 f`, and `D_2 f` exist throughout an open planar set, and `D_21 f` is continuous at `(a,b)`. Then `D_12 f` exists at that point and equals `D_21 f` there. Attached corollary: a `C^2` function has equal mixed partials. Do not strengthen the principal hypothesis to `C^2` or assume the missing mixed derivative already exists.
- **PMA-9.42** - theorem/consequence, pp.236-237: Let `phi` be defined on `[a,b] x [c,d]`, let `alpha` be increasing on `[a,b]`, and suppose each `x`-slice at fixed `t` is Riemann-Stieltjes integrable with respect to `alpha`. At interior `s`, assume the parameter derivative exists locally and approaches its value at `s` uniformly in `x` as `t -> s`. Then the derivative slice is Riemann-Stieltjes integrable, and `f(t)=integral_a^b phi(x,t) d alpha(x)` has derivative at `s` equal to the integral of that slice. Attached sufficient condition: continuity of the parameter derivative on the compact rectangle gives the required uniform property. This is the native Chapter 6 integral, not merely a Lebesgue-integral analogue.
- **PMA-9.43** - example/development, pp.237-238: The improper whole-line integrals `f(t)=integral exp(-x^2)*cos(x*t) dx` and `g(t)=-integral x*exp(-x^2)*sin(x*t) dx` converge absolutely for every real `t`. The function `f` is differentiable with `f'=g`, satisfies `2*f'(t)+t*f(t)=0`, and equals `sqrt(pi)*exp(-t^2/4)`. Include the defining improper-integral limits and convergence; a totalized integral identity alone is insufficient. The Gaussian normalization is referenced to 8.21 in the source.
