# PMA Chapter 7 inventory - Sequences and Series of Functions

Source: the local third-edition book, printed pp.143-165 (PDF pp.152-174). The chapter contains exactly **33 consecutive numbered main-text items, 7.1-7.33**: seven definitions, seven examples, eighteen theorems, and one numbered corollary. Exercises begin on printed p.165, after 7.33, and are excluded. Every relevant page was rendered and inspected in memory; neither the PDF nor extracted book text or page images were saved. This inventory gives brief mathematical paraphrases only. Independent extraction and comparison accepted this complete scope; see `evidence/ch07-source-independent-review.md`. The only change since the reviewed inventory snapshot is this review-status sentence. Concrete typed contracts still require independent review before their proofs.

The default codomain is complex, with real-valued special cases where specified. Uniform statements concern the full stated domain, not merely compact subsets. Sequence indices are positive unless specified otherwise; the series in 7.3 starts at zero. Real-parameter derivatives of complex functions in 7.17 use the Chapter 5 semantics; on a nondegenerate closed interval they include the appropriate one-sided endpoint derivatives. Integration in 7.6 and 7.16 means the native Chapter 6 Riemann/Riemann-Stieltjes integral, including its explicit complex specialization. No continuity convention is added to a nondecreasing integrator.

## Numbered nodes

- **PMA-7.1** (definition, pp.143-144) - Define pointwise convergence on a set and its limit function. Define the pointwise sum of a function series by convergence of its numerical series at each point. These assert existence of the limits/sums; totalized limit or sum operators alone are insufficient. Deps: Chapter 3 sequences and series.
- **PMA-7.2** (example, pp.144-145) - For positive integers `m,n`, the double sequence `m/(m+n)` has iterated limits 1 when `m` tends to infinity first and 0 in the reverse order. Include both inner-limit assertions and both unequal iterated limits. Deps: Chapter 3 limit algebra.
- **PMA-7.3** (example, p.145) - The continuous real functions `x^2/(1+x^2)^n`, `n>=0`, form a pointwise convergent series on the real line. Its sum is 0 at zero and `1+x^2` elsewhere, hence is discontinuous at zero. Include term continuity, series convergence, exact sum, and failure of continuity. Deps: 7.1, geometric series, Chapter 4 continuity.
- **PMA-7.4** (example, p.145) - For positive `m`, the limit as `n` tends to infinity of `cos(m! * pi * x)^(2*n)` is 1 exactly when `m! * x` is an integer, and 0 otherwise. As `m` tends to infinity these functions converge to the indicator of the rationals. The resulting function is discontinuous everywhere and is not Riemann integrable on a nondegenerate compact real interval. The main-text nonintegrability claim is included although its cited Chapter 6 exercise is not a separate node. Deps: Chapter 3 powers, rational/irrational density, trigonometric properties, Chapter 6 native integrability.
- **PMA-7.5** (example, p.146) - For `n>=1`, `sin(n*x)/sqrt(n)` converges to zero on the real line (indeed uniformly, as explicitly recalled before 7.17). Each derivative is `sqrt(n)*cos(n*x)`, while the limit's derivative is zero; at zero the derivatives tend to positive infinity. Thus even uniform convergence does not justify differentiating the limit. Deps: 7.1/7.7, Chapter 5 derivative rules, trigonometric bounds.
- **PMA-7.6** (example, pp.146-147) - On `[0,1]`, both `n^2*x*(1-x^2)^n` and `n*x*(1-x^2)^n` converge pointwise to zero. Their native integrals are respectively `n^2/(2*n+2)` and `n/(2*n+2)`, tending to positive infinity and `1/2`; the integral of the limiting zero function is zero. Include integrability and both scaling variants, demonstrating failure of interchange even when the limiting integral values are finite. Deps: Chapter 3 polynomial/geometric decay, Chapter 6 integral identities.
- **PMA-7.7** (definition/consequences, p.147) - Uniform convergence has one eventual index for all domain points at each positive tolerance. It implies pointwise convergence. A function series converges uniformly when its partial sums do so. Retain the order of the quantifiers; the source's non-strict error inequality is equivalent to the usual strict formulation. Deps: 7.1.
- **PMA-7.8** (theorem, pp.147-148) - A complex function sequence has a uniform limit on `E` iff its differences satisfy the uniform Cauchy condition: for every positive tolerance all sufficiently late pairs of indices are close at every point of `E`. The existence of a limit function is part of the forward construction from Cauchy data. Deps: 7.7 and completeness of the complex numbers.
- **PMA-7.9** (theorem, p.148) - For a sequence converging pointwise to `f`, uniform convergence to `f` is equivalent to its supremum absolute error tending to zero. The supremum must allow infinity for unbounded errors; uniform convergence need not bound every initial error function. An extended nonnegative supremum handles these cases and the empty domain; a real-supremum comparator requires explicit boundedness for the error under comparison. Deps: 7.7, completeness/suprema.
- **PMA-7.10** (theorem/caution, p.148) - If `|f_n(x)|<=M_n` for all indices and points and the numerical majorant series converges, the function series converges uniformly. Include the attached fact that the converse fails: uniform convergence need not admit a summable sequence of absolute majorants. A simple verified witness suffices; the source prescribes no particular counterexample. Deps: 7.8, Chapter 3 Cauchy/comparison criteria.
- **PMA-7.11** (theorem, p.149) - Suppose `f_n` converges uniformly on a subset `E` of a metric space, `x` is an accumulation point of `E`, and each `f_n` has a limit `A_n` as its argument tends to `x` through `E` excluding `x`. Then `A_n` converges, the limit function has the corresponding limit at `x`, and these two limits agree. No membership of `x` in `E` or continuity of the individual functions is required. Deps: 7.8, Chapter 4 relative function limits.
- **PMA-7.12** (theorem/caution, p.150) - A uniform limit of continuous functions on `E` is continuous on `E`. Include the failure of the converse even when both the approximants and pointwise limit are continuous; the functions in 7.6 supply the stated example. Deps: 7.11, 7.6.
- **PMA-7.13** (theorem/example, p.150) - On a compact set, a pointwise decreasing sequence of continuous real functions with continuous pointwise limit converges uniformly. Include the necessity-of-compactness example `1/(n*x+1)` on `(0,1)`: continuous functions decreasing pointwise to zero, with convergence not uniform. Deps: 7.7, Chapter 2 compactness and Chapter 4 continuity.
- **PMA-7.14** (definition/consequences, pp.150-151) - Define the space of bounded continuous complex functions on a metric space `X`, its supremum norm, and distance given by the norm of the difference. Include finiteness, zero-norm characterization, triangle inequality and metric structure, and equivalence of metric convergence with uniform convergence. On compact `X`, continuity implies boundedness. Define uniformly closed sets and uniform closure in this metric. Deps: 7.9, Chapter 4 compact boundedness.
- **PMA-7.15** (theorem, p.151) - The bounded continuous complex function space with the supremum metric is complete. The uniform limit produced from a Cauchy sequence remains bounded and continuous. Deps: 7.8, 7.12, 7.14.
- **PMA-7.16** (theorem/unnumbered corollary, pp.151-152) - For any nondecreasing `alpha` on `[a,b]`, a uniform limit of complex Riemann-Stieltjes integrable functions is integrable, and their integrals converge to its integral. Existence of this numerical limit is explicit. The attached corollary permits termwise integration of a uniformly convergent series of integrable functions, including convergence of the series of integrals. Deps: 7.7/7.9, Chapter 6 integral bounds, linearity and native complex integration.
- **PMA-7.17** (theorem/remark, pp.152-153) - On a nondegenerate `[a,b]`, let every `f_n` be differentiable, let `f_n(x0)` converge at some `x0` in the interval, and let the derivatives converge uniformly there. Then the functions converge uniformly to a differentiable function whose derivative is the pointwise limit of the derivatives, including interval-relative endpoints. Derivative continuity is not assumed; the following remark only describes a simpler proof under that additional assumption. Deps: 5.19, 7.8, 7.11; 7.16/FTC are optional only in the stronger continuous-derivative case.
- **PMA-7.18** (theorem, p.154) - There exists a real function on the entire real line that is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere. The theorem requires this existence statement; the particular periodic-series witness and intermediate estimates inside the source proof are not additional inventory obligations. A different fully verified witness is acceptable. Deps for the source construction: 7.10, 7.12, Chapter 5 derivatives.
- **PMA-7.19** (definition/attached consequence, p.155) - Define pointwise boundedness of a function sequence by a finite bound depending on the point, and uniform boundedness by one bound for every point and index. Include the attached selection consequence: on any countable subset of the domain, a pointwise bounded complex sequence has one subsequence converging at every point of that subset. This consequence is established by the later diagonal-selection theorem 7.23, not by the definitions alone. Deps: Chapter 3 bounded subsequences; 7.23 for the attached consequence.
- **PMA-7.20** (example, p.155) - The real sequence `sin(n*x)`, `n>=1`, consists of continuous uniformly bounded functions on `[0,2*pi]`, but has no subsequence converging pointwise everywhere on that interval. The source explicitly uses its later Theorem 11.32 in this argument; this forward dependency must be tracked rather than hidden or used to omit the example. Deps: trigonometric orthogonality and a valid bounded/dominated convergence result, or an independent proof of the same conclusion.
- **PMA-7.21** (example/attached observations, p.156) - On `[0,1]`, the continuous functions `x^2/(x^2+(1-n*x)^2)` are uniformly bounded by 1 and converge pointwise to zero, but their values at `1/n` equal 1, so no subsequence converges uniformly. Include the preceding boundedness observations: a pointwise convergent sequence of individually bounded functions need not be uniformly bounded (7.6 supplies a witness), whereas a uniformly convergent sequence of individually bounded functions is uniformly bounded. Deps: 7.6-7.9, 7.19.
- **PMA-7.22** (definition/consequences, p.156) - A family is equicontinuous in the source's sense when for every positive tolerance one positive distance bound works for every pair of domain points and every member of the family. Thus each member is uniformly continuous. Include that the family in 7.21 fails this property. This is uniform equicontinuity over `E`, not merely pointwise equicontinuity. Deps: Chapter 4 uniform continuity, 7.21.
- **PMA-7.23** (theorem, pp.156-157) - A pointwise bounded sequence of complex functions on a countable set has a subsequence converging at every point of that set. Countability includes finite and empty sets; the subsequence has a strictly increasing index map. No continuity or topology on the domain is required. Deps: Chapter 3 bounded complex subsequences and diagonal selection; 7.19's boundedness definition.
- **PMA-7.24** (theorem, p.157) - A uniformly convergent sequence of continuous complex functions on a compact metric space is equicontinuous in the source's uniform sense. Include all sequence members, not only a tail. Deps: 7.8/7.14, 7.22, compact uniform continuity.
- **PMA-7.25** (theorem, p.158) - For continuous complex functions on a compact metric space, pointwise boundedness and equicontinuity imply both uniform boundedness of the full sequence and existence of a uniformly convergent subsequence. Retain both conclusions and the weaker pointwise boundedness premise. The limit is a continuous function by 7.12. Deps: 7.8, 7.12, 7.19, 7.22-7.23, compactness and countable dense subsets of compact metric spaces.
- **PMA-7.26** (theorem, pp.159-160) - Every continuous complex function on a compact real interval is a uniform limit there of polynomial functions. For real-valued functions the polynomials can have real coefficients. Both the complex and real statements are included; the source's particular integral construction is a proof choice, not an additional conclusion. Deps for the source proof: Chapter 6 ordinary/complex integration and substitution, Chapter 4 uniform continuity, Chapter 3 decay estimates.
- **PMA-7.27** (corollary, p.161) - On every `[-a,a]` with `a>=0`, there is a sequence of real polynomials uniformly approximating absolute value, with every polynomial taking value exactly zero at zero. Do not replace exact vanishing by convergence of these values to zero. Deps: 7.26.
- **PMA-7.28** (definition/examples, p.161) - Define an algebra of complex functions by closure under addition, multiplication, and complex scalar multiplication; give the real-scalar version for real functions. A unit or all constant functions is not assumed. Define sequential uniform closedness and uniform closure as all uniform limits of sequences from the family. Include the polynomial algebra example and the reformulation of 7.26 as its uniform closure being the continuous functions on a compact interval. Deps: 7.7, 7.14, 7.26; ordinary algebra.
- **PMA-7.29** (theorem, p.161) - The uniform closure of an algebra of bounded functions is itself a uniformly closed algebra. Individual boundedness, not one common bound for the entire algebra, is the premise. Cover the real and complex versions. Deps: 7.28, uniform-limit algebra, metric closure.
- **PMA-7.30** (definition/examples, p.162) - Define separation of distinct points and vanishing at no point (at each point some family member is nonzero). Polynomial functions on the real line satisfy both; even polynomials on `[-1,1]` fail separation. Do not change pointwise existence of a nonzero member into existence of one member nonzero everywhere. Deps: polynomial evaluation, 7.28.
- **PMA-7.31** (theorem, p.162) - An algebra separating points and vanishing at no point interpolates any two prescribed values at two distinct points. Cover arbitrary complex target values for the complex algebra and arbitrary real values for the real algebra. No compactness, continuity, or unit is assumed. Deps: 7.28, 7.30, field algebra.
- **PMA-7.32** (theorem, pp.162-164) - A real algebra of continuous functions on a compact metric space, separating points and vanishing at no point, has uniform closure equal to all real continuous functions. Retain the non-unital hypotheses; adding containment of constants would weaken the assertion. The proof's lattice/interpolation steps are implementation choices, not additional numbered nodes. Deps: 7.27, 7.29-7.31, compactness.
- **PMA-7.33** (theorem/attached definition and caution, p.165) - Define self-adjointness as closure under pointwise complex conjugation. A self-adjoint complex algebra of continuous functions on a compact metric space, separating points and vanishing at no point, has uniform closure equal to all complex continuous functions (equivalently it is dense in the supremum metric). Include the attached failure of unrestricted complex Stone-Weierstrass without self-adjointness; the referenced exercise is excluded as an exercise, but the stated failure should have a verified witness. No particular exercise construction is prescribed by this inventory. Deps: 7.28-7.32, real/imaginary decomposition.

## Fidelity and dependency gates

- **Quantifiers and totalization:** source 7.9 permits infinite initial suprema; use an extended nonnegative error supremum, with real-valued comparators only when justified. Uniform convergence must have one eventual index for the entire domain. In 7.8, 7.11, 7.16, 7.17, 7.23, 7.25 and 7.26, existence/convergence is part of the conclusion, not just an identity involving a totalized operator.
- **Domains:** relative limits in 7.11 use punctured neighborhoods within `E` at an actual accumulation point. No membership of the limiting point is assumed. Derivative assertions in 7.17 concern a nondegenerate closed interval with relative endpoint derivatives; no ambient extension regularity is required. Nonderivative compact-interval assertions should retain singleton cases.
- **Complex coverage:** the chapter defaults to complex functions. A real proof of 7.8-7.12, 7.14-7.17, 7.19 or 7.23-7.26 is not by itself the full source theorem; an explicit verified complex bridge is needed. The ordered theorem 7.13 and real Stone-Weierstrass 7.32 are genuinely real statements.
- **Equicontinuity:** 7.22 requires a single distance bound uniform over all points and all family members. A pointwise-equicontinuity API needs a proved equivalence on the relevant compact domain; it cannot silently replace the definition on arbitrary sets.
- **Algebras:** 7.28 has no unit requirement. Typed contracts must state the usual nonempty-algebra convention or otherwise justify empty-domain behavior: taking the empty family as an algebra makes the unrestricted empty-space version of 7.32/7.33 false. This convention requires explicit independent review; do not silently require all constants or exclude ordinary non-unital algebras. Nowhere-vanishing and separation retain their original quantifier order.
- **Attached assertions:** the unnumbered 7.16 corollary, failure examples/cautions, exact-zero polynomial normalization in 7.27, and full real/complex distinctions remain in scope. Proof-only constructions and estimates in 7.18, 7.26 and 7.32 are not extra requirements. Exercise references do not create additional exercise nodes.
- **Actual forward dependencies:** 7.20 cites Theorem 11.32; Chapter 11 is not implemented. A pinned-library theorem or an independent proof may establish the example, but its use must be recorded honestly and must not be counted as Chapter 11 completion. Trigonometric facts used by 7.4/7.5/7.20 are also developed later in the book. No strict textbook-order import discipline is claimed. The diagonal selection attached to 7.19 is proved by 7.23; treat the boundedness definitions and that later consequence separately to avoid an artificial dependency cycle.
- **Native integrals:** 7.6 and 7.16 must refer to the accepted Chapter 6 integrals or provide an explicit equivalence. A measure-integral proof of the 7.20 counterexample needs its own justified boundary with the claimed native conclusions; no silent integral substitution is permitted.
