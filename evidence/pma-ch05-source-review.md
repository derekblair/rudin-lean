The source has nineteen numbered items, 5.1–5.19. Treat the underlying real interval as nondegenerate wherever existence of interior points or uniqueness of endpoint derivatives is used.

- **5.1 Definition.** For real `f` on `[a,b]`, the derivative at `x∈[a,b]` is the limit of `(f(t)-f(x))/(t-x)` through `a<t<b`, `t≠x`, when that limit exists. Differentiability at a point/on a subset is derivative existence. Endpoint derivatives are one-sided. If the domain is only `(a,b)`, endpoint derivatives are undefined.
- **5.2 Theorem.** Differentiability at any `x∈[a,b]`, endpoints included with the preceding convention, implies continuity relative to the domain.
- **5.3 Theorem.** Two real functions differentiable at `x∈[a,b]` have differentiable sum and product, with the usual derivative identities. Their quotient is differentiable if `g(x)≠0`, with `(g f' - g' f)/g²`.
- **5.4 Examples.** Constants have derivative zero; identity has derivative one. For every integer `n`, `x^n` has derivative `n*x^(n-1)`, requiring `x≠0` only when `n<0`. All real polynomials are differentiable, and quotients of polynomials are differentiable wherever the denominator is nonzero.
- **5.5 Theorem.** `f` is continuous on `[a,b]` and differentiable at `x∈[a,b]`; `g` is defined on an interval containing `f([a,b])` and differentiable at `f(x)`. Then `g∘f` is differentiable at `x`, with derivative `g'(f(x))*f'(x)`. Preserve interval and endpoint semantics.
- **5.6 Examples.** The zero extension of `x*sin(1/x)` has derivative `sin(1/x)-(1/x)*cos(1/x)` off zero and is not differentiable at zero. The zero extension of `x²*sin(1/x)` has derivative `2*x*sin(1/x)-cos(1/x)` off zero, derivative zero at zero, and is everywhere differentiable with derivative discontinuous at zero.
- **5.7 Definition.** For real `f` on a metric space, a local maximum at `p` means that some positive-radius open ball around `p` has every value bounded above by `f(p)`. Local minima reverse the inequality.
- **5.8 Theorem.** A local maximum or minimum of a real function at an interior point `x∈(a,b)` has derivative zero whenever the derivative exists.
- **5.9 Theorem.** Real `f,g` continuous on `[a,b]` and differentiable on `(a,b)` have an interior `x` satisfying `(f(b)-f(a))*g'(x)=(g(b)-g(a))*f'(x)`. Endpoint differentiability is unnecessary.
- **5.10 Theorem.** Real `f` continuous on `[a,b]` and differentiable on `(a,b)` has an interior `x` satisfying `f(b)-f(a)=(b-a)*f'(x)`.
- **5.11 Theorem.** For real `f` differentiable on `(a,b)`, a nonnegative derivative implies monotone increase, identically zero derivative implies constancy, and a nonpositive derivative implies monotone decrease. No endpoint continuity is assumed.
- **5.12 Theorem and unnumbered corollary.** Real `f` differentiable on `[a,b]` attains every derivative value strictly between its endpoint derivatives at an interior point, for either strict ordering of those endpoint derivatives. The explicit corollary on PDF page 118 forbids simple discontinuities of `f'` on `[a,b]`; this includes removable discontinuities and jumps, not merely jumps. Second-kind discontinuities remain possible (5.6(b)).
- **5.13 Theorem.** Real `f,g` differentiable throughout `(a,b)`, `g'≠0` there, and extended-real derivative quotient limit `A` at the left endpoint imply the same limit for `f/g`, provided either both functions tend to zero or `g` tends to positive infinity. Source endpoints may be infinite (`−∞≤a<b≤+∞`). Fold the explicitly stated right-endpoint analogue and the negative-infinity denominator analogue. No limit assumption on `f` is needed in the infinite-denominator cases; `A` may be finite or either infinity.
- **5.14 Definition.** Iterated derivatives are successive derivatives, beginning with the second derivative of `f`. Existence of order `n` at a point requires the order `n−1` derivative to exist nearby and be differentiable at that point, with one-sided neighborhoods at domain endpoints; the existence requirements propagate to lower orders.
- **5.15 Theorem and consequences.** For positive integer `n`, real `f` on `[a,b]` has its order `n−1` derivative continuous on `[a,b]` and order `n` derivative existing on `(a,b)`. For distinct `α,β∈[a,b]`, in either order, form `P(t)=Σ(k=0..n−1) f^(k)(α)*(t−α)^k/k!`. Some `x` strictly between `α` and `β` satisfies `f(β)=P(β)+f^(n)(x)*(β−α)^n/n!`. Lower derivative existence is implicit in the higher-derivative hypotheses. The case `n=1` recovers the mean-value theorem, and a bound for the highest derivative yields the usual absolute remainder bound.
- **5.16 Remarks.** The derivative definition, continuity implication, and sum/product/quotient rules extend to complex-valued functions of a real variable. Complex differentiability with respect to that real variable is equivalent to differentiability of real and imaginary parts, with derivative reconstructed from them. For curves into finite-dimensional real Euclidean space, the derivative is the limit of vector difference quotients in norm, exists iff all component derivatives exist, and has precisely those components. Continuity and sum rules persist; the scalar product has derivative `f'·g+f·g'`.
- **5.17 Example.** `f(x)=exp(i*x)=cos x+i sin x` has `f(2π)=f(0)` and derivative `i*exp(i*x)` of norm one everywhere. Hence the real scalar mean-value identity cannot hold for this curve.
- **5.18 Example.** On `(0,1)`, `f(x)=x` and `g(x)=x+x²*exp(i/x²)`. As `x→0+`, both vanish, while `f/g→1` and `f'/g'→0`. Here `g'=1+(2*x-2*i/x)*exp(i/x²)` and `g'` is nonzero throughout `(0,1)`. These unequal limits refute the complex-valued analogue of 5.13.
- **5.19 Theorem and preceding consequence.** A continuous curve `[a,b]→ℝᵏ` differentiable on `(a,b)` admits `x∈(a,b)` with `‖f(b)-f(a)‖≤(b-a)*‖f'(x)‖`. Fold the corresponding uniform-bound consequence: if all derivative norms are at most `M`, the endpoint norm is at most `(b-a)*M`; a finite supremum gives the displayed instance.
